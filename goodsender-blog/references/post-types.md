# Adapt to the post type

Read at intake, then use the relevant checks while drafting and reviewing. Infer the type from the request or source material; ask only if ambiguity would change the work. A post may combine types. These are decision criteria, not mandatory templates or an exhaustive list.

Preserve the requested length, angle, outline, and title. Draft-only is delivery intent, not a post type or a request for a short sample. An unfamiliar format still uses the common workflow; identify its factual claims, attribution needs, and promised reader outcome.

| Type | What to preserve or establish | Checks that matter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explainer or educational post | The reader's starting knowledge, question, and desired understanding | Support asserted mechanisms and causal relationships, not only numbers. Separate an illustrative model from observed behavior. |
| Announcement, release notes, or changelog | What changed, who is affected, availability, and any required action | Distinguish shipped behavior from plans. Confirm versions, dates, scope, and migration steps from the supplied evidence; a changelog line does not establish plan eligibility or customer demand. |
| Tutorial or how-to | Prerequisites, environment/version, steps, expected result, and recovery where needed | Verify instructions against documentation or supplied implementation. Run examples only in an available safe test environment within scope; never change a live system to validate an article. Distinguish executed checks, source review, and untested steps in the ledger. |
| Opinion or argument | The author's position and reasoning; counterarguments where useful | Do not demand sources for a stated preference. Check the factual premises and quotations. Avoid turning the author's view into an invented consensus, biography, or promotional CTA. |
| Personal essay or retrospective | Supplied experiences, voice, chronology, and what the author takes from them | Do not invent real scenes, dialogue, dates, motives, or outcomes. A reflective or emotional ending is a valid goal. Ask for experience only if essential material is missing. |
| Case study or customer story | The problem, intervention, baseline, outcome, period, and measurement basis where results are claimed | Separate measured outcomes from estimates and attributed testimony; do not infer causation from sequence. Use named customers or identifying private details only when cleared for publication; otherwise anonymize. Quotes need an actual supplied statement. |
| Interview, Q&A, or profile | The transcript or notes, speaker identity, and intended editing level | Keep exact quotations faithful and attributed. Present paraphrases as paraphrases. Do not manufacture answers or silently turn edited prose into a verbatim quote. Distinguish what the speaker said from independently verified claims. |
| Review, comparison, or recommendation | The reader's decision, comparable criteria, versions/date, and basis of evaluation | Separate hands-on findings, documented features, and the author's preference. Do not invent testing or a universal winner. Source changing prices and availability. Preserve supplied sponsorship, affiliate, or other relevant relationship disclosures; ask only if needed to represent the review accurately. |
| News, analysis, roundup, or curated list | The reporting cutoff, event dates, original sources, and reason each item belongs | Separate the event date from publication/update dates, reporting from interpretation, and allegations from established facts. Link to original reporting or primary sources; do not infer "latest" from an old blog post. Selection criteria need no invented ranking or exhaustive-coverage claim. |
| Fiction, satire, or other creative writing | The requested creative premise, form, narrator, tone, and intended experience | Invent scenes and characters within the authorized creative scope. Keep fictional/satirical framing clear, including metadata and announcements. Do not source every fictional sentence or treat the narrator's "I" as the author's life. Real-world factual assertions and nonfiction framing still need evidence. |

Use a title appropriate to the form: an evocative story or essay title can serve the reader without making an empirical claim. A post need not contain headings, a comparison table, author credentials, an interview, or a call to action simply because another type benefits from them.

For creative work, check coherence, continuity, voice, and the requested constraints as well as any real-world claims. When no factual claims exist, record that briefly in the ledger instead of manufacturing sources or fact-check counts.
